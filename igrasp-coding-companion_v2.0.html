b1 Prism Diagonal

Truth value: True

In a rectangular prism, the space diagonal connecting opposite corners is longer than any edge or face diagonal.

Watch for on this conjecture

  • An analogy or stand-in object ("a Kleenex box") is coded by the reasoning that follows it, so General is 1 only if that reasoning addresses all prisms rather than the imagined box.
  • Thinking of a triangle beside a recalled fact is not an operation. A construction earns Operative when the participant names the parts that build it ("a triangle of an edge, a face diagonal, and the space diagonal").
  • For Logical, the participant must also say where the diagonal sits in the construction. "Connecting the corners makes a triangle" together with "the hypotenuse is the longest side," with no word on which line is the hypotenuse, leaves the middle step to the reader and is 0.

General

Is the participant's end reasoning about the conjecture pitched at all cases?

Coded 1

any line through the 3D shape, obviously if you like, keep the diagonal along one face, it's gonna be shorter [...] I can't think of any like, possible orientation of the line where it would be longer than that.

Why. The reasoning is conducted on "any line through the 3D shape" in the participant's own words; the closing "I can't think of any possible orientation" is a report of a search and is not what earns the code.

Coded 0

I'm picturing a Kleenex box. If I were to make a line segment from the bottom corner of one side to the top corner of another side, that space diagonal is longer [...] it's got farther to go.

Why. The reasoning stays tied to the imagined box and is never extended to all prisms.

Logical

Does the participant state a deductive chain?

Coded 1

the face diagonal of the rectangular part, it's longer than any edge. And then the space diagonal is longer than the face diagonal because the face diagonal connects two points in the same plane

Why. Two dependent steps (face diagonal exceeds an edge; space diagonal exceeds the face diagonal because it leaves the plane) lead to the conclusion.

Coded 0

it's just gonna be longer going through the, like, volume of the prism versus the exterior lines or edges. It's just how it looks in my brain.

Why. A correct image is stated without any step that derives it.

Operative

Does the participant name an operation on the object and say what it produces?

Coded 1

if you have a rectangle and you just have the diagonal across that, and then as you extend that up, then you're adding, like as you add height to that original diagonal, then you're gonna be making it longer

Why. The participant extends the face diagonal upward and states the result, which is the length the conjecture is about.

Coded 0

I'm picturing a rectangular prism [...] I'm thinking of a triangle. The hypotenuse is always going to be longer than any of the two sides.

Why. A triangle is thought of beside a fact, but it is never built from the prism's parts; the diagonal is not placed in it.

Gestures

What a qualifying gesture could look like on this conjecture, described in words. Code from the hands in the video.

Dynamic Gesture
A finger drawing the diagonal as it travels from one corner, across a face, and up to the opposite corner.
Non-dynamic Gesture

b2 Polygons and Angles

Truth value: False

The interior angles of any regular polygon always sum to 360 degrees.

Watch for on this conjecture

  • A polygon named by its number of sides ("a triangle," "a hexagon") is a case specific enough to check even when the participant does not say "regular," and a loose estimate of the individual angles does not invalidate a case whose sum is in fact not 360.
  • A wrong remark beside a valid case does not undo the case: "a triangle is 180, and I don't think a square counts as a polygon" still offers a valid counterexample (Rule C4).
  • Totalling values already given ("four right angles make 360") is not an operation, although Logical may credit it as a step, because a computation whose operands and result are stated is a chain even on a single instance.

General

Is the participant's end reasoning about the conjecture pitched at all cases?

Coded 1

a Pentagon has interior angles sum at 540 [...] I know shapes above shapes that have more than five sides have more than 360 degrees.

Why. A valid counterexample, and the result is extended to the class of polygons with more sides. A polygon named by its number of sides ("a triangle," "a hexagon," "a ten-sided polygon") is a case specific enough to check even when the participant does not say "regular," because the angle sum depends only on the number of sides, and a loose estimate of the individual angles along the way does not invalidate a case whose sum is in fact not 360.

Coded 0

regular polygon, that also includes like a rectangle. So that would be, those are all 90, 99 90, which is 180 180 360 [...] I'm gonna stick with true

Why. One tested case is used as evidence with no claim that it covers the class.

Logical

Does the participant state a deductive chain?

Coded 1

for a triangle, like the interior angles sum to [...] 180 degrees. And then for a rectangle it's sum to 360 degrees. And once we increase one more side, we increase 180 more degrees to the total sum

Why. Stated cases and a rule for how the sum changes with each side form a chain to the conclusion.

Coded 0

False because they'll only sum to 360 degrees at their rectangle. So like not pentagons or other shapes.

Why. The class that fails is named, but no step shows why it fails; the claim is asserted.

Operative

Does the participant name an operation on the object and say what it produces?

Coded 1

for shapes like Pentagons it would be 540. And in general for a regular polygon of n sides, it would be 180 times in parentheses n minus two.

Why. The formula is applied and carried through to a result that bears on the conjecture.

Coded 0

I know circle's always 360, but a triangle, even a right side triangle is 180 and even an equilateral triangle is 180. So false.

Why. Facts are recalled; nothing is operated on.

b3 Pyramid Volume

  • Setting up the figure the conjecture describes is the starting state, not an operation; placing the pyramid inside the prism and saying what the remaining pieces are is an operation.

b4 Similar Triangles

Truth value: True

If two triangles have two pairs of corresponding angles that are congruent (in the same relative position), the triangles must be similar.

Watch for on this conjecture

  • This conjecture carries the General boundary most likely to need discussion: a derivation on an unspecified triangle, with no claim that it covers all triangles, is 0. Both General excerpts below find the third angle, and only the first states that it is "always determined."
  • A participant's "any" that is about something other than the conclusion, such as the meaning of the word "similar," does not make the reasoning general.
  • Subtracting the two known angles from 180 to get the third is an operation on quantities; citing the 180° rule and then stating the conclusion is not.

General

Is the participant's end reasoning about the conjecture pitched at all cases?

Coded 1

if we know just angles, then we can scale that triangle and the angles will remain the same. Two pairs of corresponding angles means a third is always determined because angles add up to 180.

Why. The conclusion is stated for all such triangles ("always determined") in the participant's words.

Coded 0

if two of the angles are congruent, then you know, just simple subtraction. Third angle must be the same as well [...] You could just scale it up or down the size of it.

Why. A derivation on an unspecified triangle with no claim that it covers all triangles; the participant's "any triangles are similar" elsewhere is about the word similar, not the conclusion.

Logical

Does the participant state a deductive chain?

Coded 1

the interior angles of a triangle do add up to 180 degrees, so then the third angle must be the same. And that is, I think what's similarity means. So true.

Why. Two dependent steps (angle sum fixes the third angle; equal angles is similarity) reach the conclusion.

Coded 0

corresponding angles [...] angles that are in the same relative position and then congruent having the same exact size measurements. So that would mean that the triangles are similar

Why. Definitions are restated and the conclusion is asserted; no step connects them.

Operative

Does the participant name an operation on the object and say what it produces?

Coded 1

if you have like one 90 and 1 45, the other has to be 45. Similar reasoning to other angles as well.

Why. The 180° rule is applied to values and carried through to a result that bears on the conjecture.

Coded 0

triangles have 180 degrees that they have two corresponding angles and the third angle has to be corresponding 'cause there's three angles.

Why. The rule is cited and the conclusion stated; nothing is carried out.

Gestures

b5 Cone Cross-section

Truth value: False

A plane that intersects a cone can only create circular or elliptical cross-sections.

Watch for on this conjecture

  • A vertical cut, whether the participant calls it down the middle, through the tip, or perpendicular to the base, gives a section that is neither a circle nor an ellipse, so naming the cut and a triangular result is a valid counterexample.
  • For Logical, the participant must say what the cut produces (a triangle, a straight edge); saying only that the result is not a circle or an ellipse shows that the conjecture fails without showing why. For Operative, by contrast, what the result is not ("not a circle or an ellipse") does count as a stated result.
  • "No matter what angle you slice" ranges over every angle at once and names no particular slice, so it is not an operation.

General

Is the participant's end reasoning about the conjecture pitched at all cases?

Coded 1

False. Like if that plane is like cut from the top, that the point to the end, and then you will get a triangle.

Why. A valid counterexample: a vertical cut, whether called down the middle, through the tip, or perpendicular to the base, gives a triangle or another section that is neither a circle nor an ellipse, so naming the cut and a triangular result is enough.

Coded 0

if the plane is [...] parallel to that flat base base, then the cut is gonna be a circular. If I tilt [...] gonna be like an oval [...] there might be not many other case that create a different shape

Why. Cases are surveyed and the conclusion is hedged; nothing valid is claimed for the class.

Logical

Does the participant state a deductive chain?

Coded 1

it's not an infinite cone [...] if you take a plane that intersects the base, then that edge will be straight and that will not be a circle or ellipse.

Why. The case is constructed and the feature that makes it fail (a straight edge) is derived.

Coded 0

the statement is false because if a plane intersects, divides the cone into exact half, then we will not get a circle or an ellipse.

Why. The case is named and the outcome asserted, but the step that shows why (what shape results) is missing.

Operative

Does the participant name an operation on the object and say what it produces?

Coded 1

if you had a cone that goes up like this, then you had a plan that slices directly down, like through the tip of the cone [...] the cross-section would kind of be triangular because the base would be flat

Why. A specific slice is carried out and its result, the cross-section, is stated.

Coded 0

no matter what angle you slice a cone at, you'll always get some kind of stretched or compressed circle shape you won't get any other. So that's true.

Why. Slicing is mentioned, but no slice is carried out; the result of all slices is asserted.

b6 Trapezoid

Watch for on this conjecture

How to work through a response

Read the whole segment once before coding anything. Then, for each form code, apply its test in the order written, apply any rule that fits the response, and compare the response with the anchor pair on the conjecture's page. The anchors show how the general rule applies; they never create a rule that holds for one conjecture only. If you are still unsure, take the code's default, which for all three codes is 0.

General

Is the participant's end reasoning about the conjecture pitched at all cases?

Test, in order

  1. Strike out the conjecture's own wording and any restatement of it. An "always" or "any" taken from the statement does not count.
  2. Find the participant's conclusion about the conjecture and the reasoning that supports it.
  3. Code 1 only if, in the participant's own words, the conclusion is stated to hold for all instances ("any triangle," "every cone"), or a tested case is explicitly extended to the whole class ("that applies to any cube"), or a false conjecture is refuted by a valid counterexample (Rule C4).
  4. Otherwise code 0.

When in doubt

Strict. Start at 0 and move to 1 only on evidence in the participant's own words, without reading in intent.

  • A property stated about the object ("a sphere is round") is not a generalization.
  • A derivation on an unspecified triangle or cube, with no claim that it covers all of them, is 0 (the b4 page).
  • "I can't think of a case where it fails" reports a search rather than making a claim about the class.

Logical

Does the participant state a deductive chain?

Test, in order

  1. List the steps the participant actually states: facts, definitions, computations, constructed cases, or if–then claims.
  2. Code 1 only if a stated premise produces an intermediate claim that is then used to reach the conclusion, so that the chain runs premise, intermediate, conclusion. The conclusion is never the intermediate, and the intermediate must say more than the premise.
  3. A valid counterexample also counts when the participant states the case and what it produces, not only that it fails (Rule C4).
  4. Otherwise code 0.

When in doubt

Strict. If you cannot point to a second step that depends on the first, code 0.

  • One reason followed by the verdict is a premise and a conclusion, not a chain, however apt the reason.
  • A correct image with no connecting steps is 0, and so is a case that is only confirmed ("I tried it and it worked").
  • Support from authority or appearance ("it's a theorem," "it looks right") is 0.

Operative

Does the participant name an operation on the object and say what it produces?

Test, in order

  1. Operation. A specific change to the object or its quantities, or a construction built from its named parts, that you could picture from the participant's words (cut through the tip, unroll the surface, double each side, place the plane at the top). It may be performed in VR, watched, or only imagined.
  2. Result. The participant states what the operation produces: a shape, size, position, or value, a direction of change, what the result is not, or that the property changed or stayed the same.
  3. Bearing. The result concerns the property the conjecture is about, not only the setup, although it need not reach the conjecture's own comparison.
  4. If all three hold, code 1; otherwise code 0.

When in doubt

Strict on presence, lenient on quality. A result that is hedged, loosely worded, revised, or wrong still counts, but a missing operation, result, or bearing does not.

  • Not an operation: picturing the figure the conjecture already describes, ranging over every position at once ("no matter where you place the chord"), reciting a relation, totalling values already given, or recalling an end state without the motion that produced it.
  • A watched manipulation earns the code on exactly the same terms as a performed one.

Codes that are easily confused

  • Logical General

    A computation whose operands and result are stated ("each angle is 90 and there are four, so 360") is a chain even on a single instance, so it can earn Logical; whether that instance covers the whole class is a separate question, which General answers.

  • Logical Operative

    A VR manipulation retold with its outcome earns Operative but not Logical, unless the participant adds steps that derive the outcome. Totalling values already given is not an operation, although Logical may credit it as a step.

  • General Operative

    "No matter where you place the chord" can earn General, because it speaks to every position, but it names no change to the chord, so it does not earn Operative.

  • Correctness Form

    The three form codes judge the kind of justification, not whether it is right, so any of them can be 1 when the content is mathematically wrong (Rule C1). The one exception is a counterexample, which must be valid (Rule C4).

Validity codes

Intuition and Insight judge whether the content is correct, not the form of the justification (Rule C1). In the re-coding pass they are carried over from the first pass.

Intuition

What was the participant's first verdict, and was it correct?

Test, in order

  1. Find the first true/false verdict stated after the statement is read.
  2. If it is explicit, score it against the key: correct = 1, incorrect = 0.
  3. If no clean verdict is stated, infer the stance from a minimal cue ("yeah," "no," "I don't think so") or from reasoning that stays consistent to the end: correct = 1_inferred, incorrect = 0_inferred.
  4. Only if nothing can be inferred, code missing.

Values

Value Use when
1 Correct verdict, stated explicitly at the outset.
0 Incorrect verdict, stated explicitly at the outset.
1_inferred No clean explicit verdict, but a correct stance is inferable, from a minimal cue ("yeah," "no," "I don't think so") or from reasoning that stays consistent through to the end.
0_inferred As above, but the inferable stance is incorrect.
missing The participant went straight into reasoning and no verdict can be inferred at all.

When in doubt

Infer before you settle on missing. Later changes of mind never alter the code; it is locked to the first verdict.

  • For i5, the verdict is scored by Rule C5, not by the key alone.

Insight

Is at least one correct, relevant mathematical idea present?

Test, in order

  1. Set aside the verdict; look at the ideas offered.
  2. Is there a valid idea, relationship, computation, or counterexample that bears on why the conjecture holds or fails? If yes, and nothing said about it is mathematically wrong, code 1.
  3. If the only content is a restatement of the conjecture, a wrong idea, or no idea, code 0.

When in doubt

Generous. If the spirit of a valid idea is there and the reasoning is not wrong, code 1, even when it is partial or loosely worded.

  • A correct verdict with no idea is 0.
  • A wrong verdict can still carry a correct idea (code 1).
  • Counterexamples are judged by Rule C4.

Gesture codes

The gesture codes are coded from the hands in the video, not from the speech (Rule C7). In the re-coding pass they are carried over from the first pass. Each conjecture page describes, in words, what a qualifying gesture might look like on that conjecture.

Dynamic Gesture

Does at least one hand gesture depict an object, or part of one, undergoing a change?

Test, in order

  1. Identify representational gesture units (Rule C7); ignore beats, self-touch, and incidental movement.
  2. For each unit, ask whether the hand does something to the imagined object: rotates, slides, folds, stretches, compresses, slices, or carries it along a path, so that the object is shown in one state and then another.
  3. If at least one unit does so, code 1; otherwise 0.

When in doubt

Code from the hands, not the speech. If you cannot see a change enacted on the object, code 0.

  • A hand moving into position to show a shape, size, or location is placing the depiction, not transforming the object; that unit is Non-dynamic.
  • Tracing along an already-existing static line or arc is Non-dynamic; tracing that carries the object or its moving part along its path is Dynamic.
  • Within one unit that both holds a form and moves to change it, Dynamic wins (Rule C7).

Non-dynamic Gesture

Does at least one hand gesture depict an object, or part of one, held static?

Test, in order

  1. Identify representational gesture units (Rule C7).
  2. For each unit, ask whether the hands show the object's shape, outline, size, or location, point to or hold a part, or trace along an already-existing static line or arc, with no transforming motion.
  3. If at least one unit does so, code 1; otherwise 0.

When in doubt

Code from the hands, not the speech; presence, not count.

  • Both codes can be 1 in the same response when separate units qualify.
  • A single unit that both holds a form and changes it is Dynamic only; a separate static unit can still earn Non-dynamic.

Rules that apply across codes

C1

The codes are independent, although correlated.

No code requires another. Intuition and Insight judge whether the content is correct; General, Logical, and Operative judge the form of the justification, following Harel and Sowder's three characteristics of the transformational proof scheme (generality, operational thought, and logical inference). The gesture codes are coded from what is observed, independently of the verbal codes and of each other.

C2

One verbal code is generous and three are strict.

Insight is coded generously. General, Logical, and Operative start at 0 and become 1 only when the defining feature is genuinely present in the participant's own reasoning; when in doubt, they stay at 0.

C3

Absence is 0.

Every code except Intuition is 0 when its feature is absent. Only Intuition may be coded missing, and only after an attempt to infer a stance has failed.

C4

Counterexamples must be valid.

A counterexample is valid only if it is mathematically correct, actually refutes the conjecture, and is named specifically enough to check (a cut through the cone's tip, or a trapezoid with one base much longer than the other), rather than left generic ("a shape where one line is steeper").

  • A valid counterexample earns General = 1. It also earns Logical = 1 when the participant states what the case produces (a resulting shape, sum, or crossing point, or a feature such as a straight edge or a corner), however loosely worded ("somewhat of a triangle," "it comes to a point"), and not only that the conjecture fails.
  • An incorrect counterexample, or one whose own description mixes correct and incorrect claims, earns neither General nor Logical.
  • Validity is judged on the case and what is claimed of it, not on the rest of the response. A wrong remark beside a valid case does not undo the case, and neither does a loose estimate along the way when the case does in fact refute the conjecture.
  • A refutation that is not a counterexample earns General only through the ordinary General test, that is, when the participant states it for all instances in their own words.
C5

Plane and Sphere (i5): the verdict depends on the reasoning.

The participant argues Intuition Insight
"False," because a tangent plane meets the sphere at a single point 1 1
"True," because a point is a degenerate circle, so the intersection is always a possibly degenerate circle (this level of specificity is required) 1 1
"True," because "wherever you cut it, it's a circle," which ignores the tangent case 0 0

For General and Logical on i5, "a sphere is round, so any cut is a circle" is 0 on both, whereas the argument that every point of the sphere is the same distance from the center, so any slice is a set of points at one distance, earns both. The i5 page gives the anchors.

C6

Code only the participant.

The researcher's reading of the statement, prompts, and encouragement never count toward any code. When the participant's speech is labeled "Researcher," or the speakers run together, use the content to tell them apart, and do not credit a passage to the participant unless the attribution is clear. Speech after the researcher has moved on to the next statement belongs to the next response.

C7

Coding gestures.

Code only representational gestures, those that depict geometric content (a shape, a part, a location, or a transformation); ignore beat gestures, self-touch, and incidental hand movement. A gesture unit begins when the hands leave rest, depicts the object or its transformation, and ends when they return to rest; onset and offset are treated as visibly recognizable. Each gesture code asks whether at least one qualifying unit occurs anywhere in the response; the number of gestures does not matter. If a single unit both traces or holds a static form and moves to change it, code that unit Dynamic; a separate static unit can still earn Non-dynamic. Determine gestures as far as possible from the hand movement and the action context alone, relying on the accompanying speech as little as possible.

Decision trees

Each tree is the code's test drawn as a sequence of yes-or-no questions. When you cannot answer "yes" on the evidence of the participant's own words, answer "no." The trees are for learning the tests and for settling a response you are unsure about; once the tests are familiar, the Codes view is faster. Pick the conjecture you are coding, and each tree shows that conjecture's anchors beneath it, so the examples are at hand while you decide.

General

  1. Start. Strike out the conjecture's own wording and any restatement of it. Find the participant's conclusion and the reasoning that supports it.

    Watch for. An "always" or "any" taken from the conjecture does not count, and neither does a restatement of the conjecture. A response that restates the conjecture and then reasons only about particular cases is 0.

  2. G1 In the participant's own words, is the conclusion stated to hold for all instances?

    Yes → leads to General = 1 No → leads to go to G2

    Watch for. A property stated about the object ("a sphere is round," "the diagonal goes through three dimensions") is not by itself a generalization, even when the verdict is confident; it earns General only when the participant uses it to conclude for all instances. A derivation on an unspecified triangle or cube, with no claim that it covers all such objects, is 0 (the b4 page). "I can't think of a case where it fails," after trying cases, reports a search. An analogy ("a Kleenex box") is coded by the reasoning that follows it.

  3. G2 Is a tested case explicitly extended to the whole class?

    Yes → leads to General = 1 No → leads to go to G3

    Watch for. The extension must be explicit ("it works for one by one, and that applies to any cube"); the same tested case without it is 0 (the i2 page). The claim about the class earns the code whether or not it is correct.

  4. G3 Is the conjecture false, and is it refuted by a valid counterexample?

    Yes → leads to General = 1 No → leads to General = 0

    Watch for. Validity follows Rule C4: mathematically correct, actually refuting the conjecture, and named specifically enough to check. An incorrect counterexample, or one whose description mixes correct and incorrect claims, earns neither General nor Logical, whereas a wrong remark beside a valid case does not undo the case. A refutation that is not a counterexample ("the width is always the circumference, which is pi times the diameter, not twice it") is judged at G1.

Logical

  1. Start. List the steps the participant actually states: facts, definitions, computations, constructed cases, or if–then claims.

  2. L0 Does the reasoning rest on a counterexample?

    Yes → leads to go to L0a No → leads to go to L1

    Watch for. This branch applies Rule C4. A response without a counterexample goes straight to the chain test at L1.

  3. L0a Is the counterexample valid, that is, correct, actually refuting the conjecture, and named specifically enough to check?

    Yes → leads to go to L0b No → leads to Logical = 0

    Watch for. An invalid counterexample earns neither General nor Logical (Rule C4).

  4. L0b Does the participant state what the case produces, such as a shape, sum, crossing point, straight edge, or corner?

    Yes → leads to Logical = 1 No, only that it fails → leads to go to L1

    Watch for. The result may be loosely worded ("somewhat of a triangle," "it comes to a point"). Saying only that the case fails, or that the result is not a circle or an ellipse, does not state what the case produces (the b5 page); such a response goes on to L1 and is checked for a chain like any other.

  5. L1 Is a premise stated, that is, a fact, definition, computation, case, or if–then claim?

    Yes → leads to go to L2 No → leads to Logical = 0

    Watch for. Specific numbers do not make reasoning empirical: a computation whose operands and result are stated ("each angle is 90 and there are four, so 360") supplies a step even on a single instance. A stated step counts even when the participant credits it to memory or a lesson, whereas authority offered in place of steps ("it's a theorem") or appearance ("it looks right") is not a premise. Steps need not all be correct.

  6. L2 Does the premise produce a stated intermediate claim that says more than the premise and is not the conclusion?

    Yes → leads to go to L3 No → leads to Logical = 0

    Watch for. The conclusion, which is the conjecture's claim or its denial, is never the intermediate. A general property followed directly by the verdict ("a sphere is round, so any cut is a circle") has no intermediate, and neither does one reason followed by the verdict, however apt. A case that is only confirmed, with its outcome reported rather than derived ("I pictured a square and it's 360"), has no intermediate. A construction is a step when the participant builds the figure, places the target in it ("the diagonal is its hypotenuse"), and gives the fact that yields the conclusion; if the placement is not said, the middle step is left to the reader and the answer is no.

  7. L3 Is that intermediate claim then used to reach the conclusion?

    Yes → leads to Logical = 1 No → leads to Logical = 0

    Watch for. The conclusion may be the verdict the participant has already given, when the intermediate is offered as its reason. A VR observation retold with its outcome is Operative, not Logical, unless the participant adds steps. A complete chain built on a misreading of the conjecture is Logical = 1 with Insight = 0, which is rare.

Operative

  1. Start. Look for something done to the object, and for what it produces. All three checks must hold.

  2. O1 Operation: does the participant name a specific change to the object or its quantities, or a construction built from its named parts, that you could picture from their words? It may be performed in VR, watched, or only imagined.

    Yes → leads to go to O2 No → leads to Operative = 0

    Watch for.

    Counts as an operation:

    • a change the participant makes to the object or its quantities (cut through the tip, unroll the surface, double each side);
    • a plane, line, or point placed at a stated position ("at the very top it'd be a point");
    • a construction whose parts are named ("a perpendicular from the center to the chord");
    • an operation on quantities that derives a value the case did not supply (subtracting the two known angles from 180 to get the third);
    • a VR manipulation, performed or watched, retold with the motion named ("as I pulled the blue line closer"), even if the mover is named loosely ("it") when the task leaves no doubt.

    Does not count:

    • picturing or drawing the figure the conjecture already describes;
    • a figure thought of beside a recalled fact;
    • naming a kind of case ("a trapezoid whose sides are angled differently") or a change only by its effect ("modify it so they don't bisect");
    • ranging over all positions at once ("no matter where you place the chord");
    • a conditional that describes the figure's own state ("if there were no top side") or how the property runs with that state ("larger angles when the lines are farther apart");
    • reciting a relation or totalling values already given ("four right angles make 360");
    • recalling an end state, or that something was manipulated, without the motion that produced it.

  3. O2 Result: does the participant state what that operation produces?

    Yes → leads to go to O3 No → leads to Operative = 0

    Watch for. A result may be a shape, size, position, or value, a direction of change ("more space left"), what the result is not ("not a circle or an ellipse"), or that the property changed or stayed the same. It may be hedged, loosely worded, revised, or wrong. A menu of possible outcomes that is not tied to the named operation, or a doubt in place of an outcome ("who's to say where the midpoint is"), is not a result.

  4. O3 Bearing: does the result concern the property the conjecture is about, not only the setup?

    Yes → leads to Operative = 1 No → leads to Operative = 0

    Watch for. A result that describes only the setup ("the sides doubled," "it formed a rectangle") does not bear on the property. The result need not reach the conjecture's own comparison: "the width stays the same" concerns the width.
